### 2. 连锁故事

On a chilly **Saturday** (星期六) morning, Professor Iris, a renowned **prophet** (先知) of etymology, sat by her window with a **quilt** (被子) wrapped around her knees. She was preparing a lecture on ancient words, and her notes were **provided** (提供的) by her assistant, who had carefully sorted every card in **proximate** (最接近的) order of their historical origin.

She began with the **qualitative** (质的、定性的) analysis of the word *quarantine* (检疫), which once meant a **forty**-day isolation for ships — a practice that saved many ports. Holding a **quart** (夸脱) of hot tea, she smiled at how a simple measure of liquid could be linked to a **queer** (奇怪的、古怪的) twist of history. Her cat, a lazy tabby, began to **quiver** (颤抖) as the wind rattled the window frame, and Iris reached for her **razor** (剃刀) to trim a loose thread from the quilt.

Turning to geometry, she sketched a **rectangle** (矩形) on the blackboard, explaining how its right angles **regarding** (关于) symmetry were crucial in architecture. She then pointed to a photo of her late grandfather, whose **remains** (遗体) rested in a small urn — a reminder that even words have a life cycle, much like a **reptile** (爬行动物) that sheds its skin. The **resultant** (结果) of her research was a new theory: every word carries a **rim** (边) of meaning that can be expanded or eroded.

As the clock struck noon, she stood to **salute** (致敬、敬礼) her students, who had gathered outside the window for a surprise visit. They held a banner that read “Words Live Forever,” and one student playfully waved a pair of **scissors** (剪刀) to cut the ribbon of a new book. The book, titled *The Ninth Month*, was dedicated to **September** (九月), the month when her journey into etymology began — a month that, in ancient Rome, was actually the **seventh** month, a fact that made her laugh at the **prophetic** (预言的) irony of time.
